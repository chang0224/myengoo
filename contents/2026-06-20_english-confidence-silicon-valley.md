#### ¶1

I didn't study English at all. And now you're a professor at a major university here. And at Yahoo, I was promoted four years in a row. You don't have to speak perfect English. People don't care who you are. Just express it. My name is Kyoung Han. I currently live in San Jose, California. It'll be 26 years as of next June.

#### ¶2

I take it you like living in California then? Yeah. Whenever I come to San Francisco Airport from Korea, I feel liberated. No one cares about me. Second, the weather is so good. Got to love that California weather. What first brought you to California?

#### ¶3

I majored in computer science back in Korea, got my bachelor's and master's, and started my career at Samsung Electronics as a Windows application developer. I stayed there for five years, and it was actually a military obligation. It was supposed to end in May 2000. So I was looking for my next move from the summer of '99. My classmate from college went to Stanford for a master's, co-founded a company called MySimon, sold it to CNET for $700 million, and then started his second company. That's when he offered me a chance to join his second startup.

#### ¶4

I didn't have any plan to live outside Korea at all. Because of that, I didn't study English at all. So when I moved in June 2000, I wasn't really ready in terms of language. Along the way, I made a lot of mistakes, and I still do. Many of them were situational: a combination of language, culture, and new situations I had never been in. One day at In-N-Out, they asked whether I wanted grilled onion or chopped onion. I just said yes, yes, yes, yes. I developed drive-thru phobia since then, and even now I get a little nervous at drive-thrus.

#### ¶5

The first thing I realized is that listening is far more important than speaking. Writing is always hard, but these days, with AI, it is not a big problem anymore. Back then, I thought I needed to improve listening first. Otherwise, I could not understand what others were saying, and then I could not react. I spent much more time improving my listening. Friends was very popular, so I practiced dictation and mirroring with it. But if you do not know the words or expressions, your hearing is not going to improve.

#### ¶6

Sometimes people pronounce things very quickly, like out of becoming outta. To make things even worse, there are many diverse ethnic groups here. Especially as a developer, you work with many Chinese and Indian colleagues. Now I can understand Chinese and Indian accents without any problem, but back then it was one of the hardest things. Improving listening is partly about learning more words and expressions, being exposed to many conversations, and interacting with diverse groups of people so that you can get used to them. It is just a matter of time.

#### ¶7

It is impressive to hear that you first came here and found a drive-thru difficult, and now you are a professor at a major university, speaking in front of many students and giving lectures. Would you say your confidence has really bloomed? Yes. At first, I was shy and worried about whether I was conveying my ideas, especially in English. Over time, I developed more confidence and thicker skin. If I do not understand, I just ask people to repeat it one more time. If I feel they are not understanding me, I repeat what I am trying to convey.

#### ¶8

Language is a means to communicate. Perfect pronunciation and syntax would be even better, but more important are tone, manner, body language, and attitude. All of these combine when you interact with other people. That took a while to learn, and it made me more confident about speaking English. You do not have to be perfect 100% of the time. If you can get your message across, then you are successful.

#### ¶9

I often talk to Korean founders who want to enter the global market, especially the US market. They are usually concerned about two things: the visa and the language. Many say they cannot speak good English, but they need to convince VCs, potential customers, and future talent. I tell them that as long as they can convey their vision and mission, that is what matters. In Silicon Valley, two-thirds of founders were not born in America. It is a majority immigrant community. Some speak very good English and some do not, but I have seen non-native founders with imperfect English become very successful. I call this aura English: speaking with confidence even if your English is a little broken.

#### ¶10

My daughter once corrected my English. I had been saying back in the days as a plural expression, not back in the day. She told me, Dad, I can understand what you are saying, but back in the days is not natural. Back in the day is more natural. It was a big shock because I had used that expression for over a decade and no one had corrected me. I asked her to tell me if she noticed more unnatural expressions. She said she would have to interrupt me every other sentence, and then we could not have a proper conversation. But she also said, Dad, you can convey your ideas. That is what matters.

#### ¶11

In 2000, I moved to Silicon Valley and worked for a web search startup my friend co-founded. It was acquired by another company, LookSmart. After six months, the company decided to let go of the entire search division, so I prepared for interviews and joined Yahoo in February 2004. At that time, I had our first kid, no green card, and not much money. That made me hungry. At Yahoo, I met great managers and co-workers, was promoted four years in a row, became a director of engineering, and learned interviewing, hiring, onboarding, and giving feedback. After seven years, I left because Yahoo was no longer doing well. I joined another startup, but it went bankrupt, so I took a one-year break.

#### ¶12

That gap year helped me stop, reflect, and think about the second half of my career. I realized that finding a place where I could make an impact was more important than finding a dream. I decided to work only for smaller companies and startups. I later joined Udemy and stayed there for four years. Since then, I have taken another break, taught at San Jose State in the Applied Data Intelligence master's program, done angel investments and career coaching, and written books. Recently I published Leadership Practice, based on lessons I had written down over two decades.

#### ¶13

Leadership is not a born talent. It is more of a skill, which means you have to practice. Along the way, you will make mistakes. As long as you do not repeat the same mistake over and over, that is fine. If you say you will become a good leader in six months, you will fail and blame your talent, not your persistence or consistency. Have a longer time horizon and build small habits that can compound over time. Strong motivation can only go so far. Be composed and consistent, and it will compound into lasting success.

#### ¶14

This applies to learning a language as well. Ten or thirty minutes a day over a few years will make your language much better. As an individual contributor, your English may not be that important. But once you become a manager, you have to convey feedback. The words and expressions you choose become very important. I paid attention to how good managers spoke in meetings and gave feedback. I also read business leadership books, especially Crucial Conversations and Crucial Confrontations. They taught me useful expressions, so I wrote them down, memorized them, and rehearsed them before giving feedback.

#### ¶15

In America, especially in Silicon Valley, many people have big dreams and are not shy about expressing what they want. Korean people, in general, do not act like that. If you want to be successful and recognized here, you have to talk about your ambition and dream. Do not limit yourself or be too self-conscious. America is more individualistic, while Korea is more collectivist. In Korea, people often care about others' opinions and go where others are going. That difference also affects work-life balance.

#### ¶16

In Korea, many people define work-life balance as working in the office and not working outside the office. The office becomes a boundary. In America, at least in my experience, people do not create that kind of boundary. If there is important work, people may work outside the office. At the same time, if your child is sick or you are sick, it is completely okay to work from home, go home early, or report to work late. Where you are during work hours does not matter as much. If you are motivated, people care less about the number of hours. Compensation can also be dramatically different. In Korea, working longer hours and achieving more does not necessarily translate into much better compensation, but in America there can be a big difference.

#### ¶17

Many companies are now talking about going global. Depending on the domain you are aiming for, the Korean market may be big, but often it is not big enough. If you are going to suffer as a founder, you might as well suffer for a bigger market and a bigger opportunity. In Korea, if you start with a small domain, you often need to add many features to cover a wider horizon. In America, you do not have to do that because even a small segment can be big enough. That is an important mindset change. Also, because Korea has many regulations, founders may not think outside the box. When they move to America, they may realize that some of those regulations do not exist.

#### ¶18

For now, I will continue working as an adjunct professor at San Jose State. Another potential project is writing a novel by turning the lessons from my leadership book into characters and a plot so that people can relate to them better. If it goes well, maybe it could become a cartoon. Thank you for inviting me. To the viewers, you do not have to speak perfect English. Be persistent and consistent. Time is the most important factor. If you stay consistent, it will compound over time and your language will become much better. Do not give up. Continue to work and study.
