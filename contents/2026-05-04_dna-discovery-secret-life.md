# DNA and the Discovery of 'The Secret of Life'

#### ¶1

It was in a pub named The Eagle, in the center of Cambridge in the UK, that one lunchtime in 1953 a scientist announced he had "found the secret of life."

#### ¶2

It wasn't the first audacious statement made in a pub, and it won't be the last. But it may have been the most important.

#### ¶3

The man with the audacious statement was Francis Crick, although he later said he didn't remember that moment — but the story was shared by his research partner James Watson.

#### ¶4

Crick and Watson had made a momentous discovery: they had worked out the chemical structure of DNA.

#### ¶5

This was the structure as we know it today — the 3D double helix, which looks like a spiral staircase and contains the genetic information that makes every living thing what it is.

#### ¶6

Crick, who was English, and the American Watson, had been studying genes at the Cavendish Laboratory at the University of Cambridge.

#### ¶7

Their discovery has been described as one of the most important in history. But it came after almost 100 years of research from other scientists.

#### ¶8

For one, Crick and Watson didn't discover DNA — Swiss scientist Friedrich Miescher did in 1869.

#### ¶9

In the first half of the 20th century, our understanding of DNA's structure was developed by Phoebus Levene of Russia and then Erwin Chargaff of Austria.

#### ¶10

And the work of Crick and Watson was supported by research from Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin. In January 1953, Wilkins showed Watson an X-ray photo that Franklin had yet to publish from her research that helped determine the double helix shape of DNA.

#### ¶11

Franklin died in 1958, while Crick, Watson and Wilkins won the Nobel Prize in 1962.

#### ¶12

So why was the double helix important enough to interrupt The Eagle's customers on February 28, 1953?

#### ¶13

Well, they had found out how genetic instructions were kept inside living things and passed from one generation to the next.

#### ¶14

This has helped in things like forensic science, treating diseases, identifying human remains and — scientists hope — very much more to come.
